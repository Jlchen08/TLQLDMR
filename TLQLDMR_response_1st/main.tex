\subsubsection*{\underline{\textbf{Reviewer 1, Concern 1}}}
\emph{The manuscript's novelty is promising, but the methodological contribution is not yet isolated clearly enough from existing SVQR, LDMR, and transfer-learning frameworks. The paper should provide a sharper theoretical comparison showing exactly what is new in TL-QLDMR beyond combining known components, and why the proposed combination is not simply an engineering integration of established ideas.}

{\color{black}
\subsubsection*{\textbf{\underline{Author response}}}
Thank you for asking us to isolate the novelty beyond combining SVQR, LDMR, and transfer learning. The revised manuscript now states explicitly why the combination is necessary rather than engineering integration: LDMR variance is restricted to source-domain centered residuals and co-optimized with $\tau$-dependent pinball asymmetry for quantile calibration plus spread control; MMD is reformulated as a projected discrepancy on the predictive function rather than full unsupervised feature alignment; and scarce target labels are supervised with $C_T \gg C_S$ to prevent source domination. Related Work now contrasts each baseline on what it lacks, and Section~3 clarifies the division of labor between pinball calibration, variance compression, and dual-space adaptation.

\subsubsection*{\underline{\textbf{Reviewer 1, Concern 2}}}
\emph{The definition of ``extreme events'' is somewhat heuristic and may bias the target-domain construction. The use of 98th-percentile thresholds, temperature cutoffs, and a fixed 25 m/s cut-out rule should be justified more rigorously, and the authors should include a sensitivity analysis showing how results change under alternative extreme-event definitions.}

{\color{black}
\subsubsection*{\textbf{\underline{Author response}}}
Thank you for raising this important point about the extreme-event definition and for suggesting a sensitivity analysis; we agree that the target-domain construction deserves a rigorous justification because it conditions every downstream comparison. The revised Section~4.2 now states the definitional basis directly: the percentile rules follow the conventional rarity-based definition of extreme meteorological states in wind-energy studies, isolating about the rarest 2\% of states at each site in a farm-adaptive way that preserves cross-site comparability, while 25\,m/s is the nominal turbine cut-out speed marking shutdown nonlinearity. Ramp events remain excluded to avoid mixing control-induced curtailment with weather-driven anomalies. We fully acknowledge your intuition that a different percentile or a different wind-speed cutoff would change the screened sample set and could shift absolute metric values. The reason we do not pursue a full retraining-based sensitivity in this revision is not that such variation is uninformative, but that it answers a different question from the one this paper asks. The focus of this work is, given a fixed and physically interpretable extreme-event target domain, whether the proposed quantile transfer mechanism improves calibration and sharpness over existing baselines under severe shift and label scarcity. Varying the definition would redefine the target population itself, so each alternative threshold would require rebuilding the target set and retraining TL-QLDMR together with all nine point-prediction and ten interval baselines in parallel, i.e.\ a complete experimental track alongside the reported one rather than a perturbation check on the same results. To keep the comparison fair and the scope focused, we therefore retain the adopted conventional definition and state alternative thresholds and per-type breakdowns as a limitation in the Conclusion, where they can motivate dedicated follow-up work.

\subsubsection*{\underline{\textbf{Reviewer 1, Concern 5}}}
\emph{The asymmetric variance regularizer is an interesting idea, but its mathematical justification remains incomplete. {\color{black}
\subsubsection*{\textbf{\underline{Author response}}}
Thank you for asking why the variance form is quantile-compatible and whether positive-definiteness and convexity hold universally. The revised manuscript now states the division of labor honestly: the variance term is a symmetric second-moment penalty on source-domain centered residuals and carries no $\tau$; quantile asymmetry comes from the weighted pinball loss, which calibrates location and skewness, while variance compresses spread. We therefore use ``asymmetric'' only in this joint sense (a quantile-compatible spread regularizer). On theory, we no longer claim universal guarantees: with $\mathbf{S}\succeq 0$, $\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{m}\mathbf{m}^T\succeq 0$, $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ is positive definite and invertible only when $\boldsymbol{K}$ is positive definite (e.g.\ RBF on distinct samples) with $\lambda_1,\lambda_2\ge 0$; with a singular $\boldsymbol{K}$ the exact objective is still convex in $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ but invertibility and strong convexity are not claimed, and nonsmooth pinball terms use subgradients.

\emph{The manuscript assigns a substantially larger penalty weight to the target domain than to the source domain in order to prevent the large number of source-domain samples from dominating joint training. For example, for Farm 6, the source domain contains 66,009 samples, whereas the target domain contains 4,144 samples, resulting in an approximate sample-size ratio of 16 to 1. The existing ``w/o Transfer'' and ``w/o MMD'' ablation studies cannot independently identify the contribution of this weighting mechanism.
